# Mr. Raga's music degree

This is the knowledge a Bachelor of Music graduate in Indian music has, with the Western theory core a BMus also teaches. The checklist follows the usual Indian university BMus syllabi (Carnatic or Hindustani, vocal or instrumental): theory, notation, history and musicology, forms, instruments, aesthetics, Western harmony, and applied music. Mr. Raga uses it to answer music questions, check facts and suggest music Shorts. The day-to-day music brief rules are in music.md.

## 1. Sound and pitch: nada, swara and shruti

- **Nada** is musical sound. The texts divide it into ahata (struck, audible) and anahata (unstruck, the mystical inner sound).
- **The seven swaras**: Sa (shadja), Ri/Re (rishabha), Ga (gandhara), Ma (madhyama), Pa (panchama), Dha (dhaivata), Ni (nishada). Sa and Pa are fixed (achala); the other five can move (chala).
- **The 12 pitch positions (swarasthanas)**:
  - Hindustani: komal (flat) Re, Ga, Dha and Ni; tivra (sharp) Ma; all the rest are shuddha (natural).
  - Carnatic uses 16 names for those 12 positions, because some positions have two names: R1, R2 = G1, R3 = G2, G3; M1, M2; D1, D2 = N1, D3 = N2, N3. For example, R2 and G1 are the same pitch, and the name used depends on the raga. This is how the 72 melakartas are counted.
- **Shruti**: the old texts divide the octave into 22 shrutis, the smallest pitch steps the ear can tell apart in music. Bharata's Natyashastra assigns 4, 3 and 2 shrutis to the gaps between the notes (Sa 4, Ri 3, Ga 2, Ma 4, Pa 4, Dha 3, Ni 2). In practice it means that a raga's notes are tuned by ear, often by just intonation (ratios such as Pa = 3:2 and Ga = 5:4), not by the piano's equal temperament. The same "komal Re" sits slightly differently in Todi and in Bhairav.
- **Saptak (octave registers)**: mandra (low), madhya (middle) and tara (high). Ati-mandra and ati-tara lie beyond them.
- **Grama and murchhana**: the ancient parent scales (shadja grama and madhyama grama) and their rotations, the murchhanas. They are the ancestors of the modal idea behind thaats and melakartas.
- **The tanpura**: four strings tuned Pa-Sa-Sa-low Sa (or Ma or Ni for some ragas). Its jawari bridge makes the rich overtone "buzz" against which every note is heard.

## 2. Raga grammar (raga lakshana)

- A raga has at least five notes. Classes by note count: audava (5 notes), shadava (6), sampurna (7), with mixtures such as audava-sampurna (5 going up, 7 coming down).
- **Arohana / avarohana**: the rising and falling order of notes. **Vakra** ragas zigzag. **Varja** notes are left out.
- **Vadi and samvadi**: the most important note and the second most important, usually a 4th or 5th apart. **Anuvadi** notes are allowed; **vivadi** notes are dissonant and used sparingly.
- **Nyasa** is a resting note where phrases end. **Graha** is the starting note. **Amsa** is the dominant note, in the old texts.
- **Pakad** (Hindustani) and **sanchara / prayoga** (Carnatic) are the signature phrases that identify a raga at once. **Chalan** is a raga's way of moving.
- **Purvanga and uttaranga**: the lower and upper tetrachords. Ragas whose vadi sits in the lower half are said to be sung from noon to midnight, and those with the vadi in the upper half from midnight to noon (the time theory).
- **Raganga**: ragas grouped by a shared core phrase (the Kanada, Malhar, Sarang and Todi families).
- **Ornaments**:
  - Hindustani: meend (glide), kan (grace note), murki (quick turn), khatka (a cluster), andolan (slow swing, as on Darbari's komal Ga), gamak (a heavy shake), zamzama.
  - Carnatic: the 10 gamakas (dasavidha gamaka): arohana, avarohana, dhalu, sphurita, kampita, ahata, pratyahata, tripuccha, andola and murchhana. In practice, kampita (oscillation), jaru (slide), nokku (a stressed touch from above), odukkal and orikai are the ones named most. Carnatic ragas are defined as much by their gamakas as by their notes.
- **Raga classification systems**: the raga-ragini family system (the old north-Indian scheme of 6 male ragas and their wives and sons, as painted in ragamala paintings), Bhatkhande's 10 thaats (20th century), Venkatamakhin's 72 melakartas (1660), and the janya ragas derived from each mela (upanga ragas use only the parent's notes; bhashanga ragas add a foreign note).

## 3. Rhythm (tala and laya)

- **Laya** is tempo: vilambit or chowka (slow), madhya (medium), drut (fast). Layakari means playing in multiples: dugun (×2), tigun (×3), chaugun (×4), and harder ones such as aad (3/2) and kuad (5/4).
- **Hindustani tala**: a theka (the tabla's basic bol pattern) in vibhags (sections). Each section is marked by a tali (clap) or a khali (wave, "empty"). **Sam** is beat 1, where everything lands. Teentaal = 4+4+4+4, with claps on 1, 5 and 13 and the khali on 9. Rhythmic devices: tihai (a phrase played 3 times to land on sam), mukhda (the lead-in phrase), chakradhar (a tihai of tihais), and the kaida, rela, tukda and paran (tabla compositions).
- **Carnatic tala**: the Suladi Sapta talas are 7 talas (Dhruva, Matya, Rupaka, Jhampa, Triputa, Ata, Eka). Each is built from angas: laghu (a clap plus finger counts), drutam (a clap and a wave) and anudrutam (a single clap). The laghu's length depends on the jati: tisra (3), chatusra (4), khanda (5), misra (7) or sankeerna (9). 7 talas × 5 jatis = 35 talas. Adi tala is chatusra-jati Triputa: 4+2+2 = 8 beats.
- **Gati or nadai** is the subdivision of each beat (tisra = 3, chatusra = 4, khanda = 5, misra = 7, sankeerna = 9). **Eduppu** is where a song starts in the cycle (on the beat, or before or after it). **Korvai** and **mora** are Carnatic cadential patterns, like the tihai. **Konnakol** is rhythm recited as syllables (ta ka di mi).
- **Chapu talas** (Misra Chapu 3+4, Khanda Chapu 2+3) come from folk and dance; they are counted, not shown with angas.

## 4. Notation

- **Bhatkhande notation** (Hindustani): notes as letters (S R G M P D N). A komal note is underlined, the tivra Ma has a vertical stroke above it, a dot below means the low octave and a dot above means the high octave. Beats go in vibhag boxes, with × for sam, 0 for khali, and the clap numbers.
- **Paluskar notation**: an alternative Hindustani system, with its own symbols for octaves and tala.
- **Carnatic notation**: s r g m p d n with the variant numbers (R1, G3 and so on), a dot for the octave, a semicolon or comma for a held beat, and the tala angas marked with | and ||. Speed is shown by underlining or brackets.
- **Western staff notation**: the treble and bass clefs, key signatures, time signatures, note values from the whole note to the 32nd, and dynamics from pp to ff. A BMus also teaches transcribing a raga phrase into staff notation (with microtonal marks where needed).

## 5. History and musicology

- **Vedic (about 1500 to 500 BCE)**: the Samaveda sets Rigveda verses to chant on three, then up to seven, pitches. It is the oldest root of Indian art music.
- **Natyashastra (Bharata, about 200 BCE to 200 CE)**: the theory of drama and music. It covers rasa, the 22 shrutis, grama and murchhana, jati (the forerunner of raga), and the four classes of instrument.
- **Brihaddeshi (Matanga, about the 9th century)**: the first text to use the word **raga** in its technical sense and to describe "desi" (regional) music.
- **Gita Govinda (Jayadeva, 12th century)**: devotional poems sung to named ragas and talas. It is still sung in Odissi and in temples.
- **Sangita Ratnakara (Sarngadeva, 13th century)**: the great summary text, honoured by both North and South. Around and after it the two traditions slowly diverged, with Persian influence in the North (the Delhi Sultanate, then the Mughals).
- **Amir Khusrau (1253 to 1325)**: the poet-musician of the Delhi Sultanate. Tradition credits him with qawwali, tarana and the khayal and with some instruments; scholars treat many of these credits as legend.
- **Bhakti and Haridasa (15th to 16th century)**: Purandara Dasa (1484 to 1564), "the father of Carnatic music", built the teaching method still used: start in Mayamalavagowla with sarali varisai, janta varisai and alankaras, then geethams.
- **Mughal courts (16th to 18th century)**: Tansen at Akbar's court (the dhrupad singer of legend; his descendants are the Senia lineage), Swami Haridas, then the rise of khayal (Sadarang and Adarang at Muhammad Shah's court) and the sitar and sarod as concert instruments.
- **Chaturdandi Prakashika (Venkatamakhin, 1660)**: the 72-melakarta scheme. Govindacharya later gave the melas their names and fixed the full-scale rule.
- **The Carnatic Trinity (18th to 19th century, Thiruvarur)**: Tyagaraja (Telugu kritis, devotion to Rama, bhava-rich), Muthuswami Dikshitar (Sanskrit kritis, intricate, Western "nottuswaras" based on band tunes, raga mudras), and Syama Sastri (rhythmic complexity, swarajatis). Swati Tirunal of Travancore was a royal composer of the same period.
- **Gharanas (Hindustani schools of style, mostly 19th to 20th century)**: in vocal music, Gwalior (the oldest khayal gharana), Agra, Jaipur-Atrauli, Kirana, Patiala, Rampur-Sahaswan, Mewati, Bhendibazaar and Indore; in instrumental music, Maihar (sitar, sarod, surbahar), Senia, Imdadkhani and Etawah (sitar); in tabla, Delhi, Ajrada, Lucknow, Farukhabad, Benares and Punjab.
- **The 20th-century reform**: V. N. Bhatkhande (the thaat system, collecting thousands of compositions, notation), V. D. Paluskar (the Gandharva Mahavidyalaya music schools and the public concert), the Madras Music Academy (1928) and the December Season, and All India Radio (1936 onwards) as the great patron. Then the global era: sitar and tabla on world stages from the 1950s to the 1970s, and fusion.
- **Film music**: it starts with Alam Ara (1931, the first Indian talkie) and soon became the country's main popular music. The eras are in music.md §7.
- **Musicology and ethnomusicology**: fieldwork, transcription, organology (the study of instruments), the guru-shishya parampara (teacher-disciple lineage) against the university system, and caste and community histories of hereditary musicians (Mirasis, Manganiyars, the Isai Vellalar nadaswaram families and the Devadasi traditions). Treat these respectfully and with care for the facts.

## 6. Composition forms

- **Carnatic, in teaching order**: sarali varisai → janta varisai → alankaras (in the 7 talas) → geetham → swarajati → varnam (tana varnam, and the dance pada varnam) → kriti or kirtanam.
- **The parts of a kriti**: pallavi (refrain), anupallavi, charanam, and sometimes chittaswaram (composed sargam passages). Its improvised parts are alapana (free raga exposition), niraval (one line of text improvised in many ways) and kalpana swaram (improvised sargam that returns to the line).
- **The Carnatic concert (kutcheri)**: an opening varnam, kritis growing in weight, a main piece with alapana, niraval and swaras, ragam-tanam-pallavi (RTP), the thani avartanam (the percussion solo), then light pieces (tukkadas: javali, padam, tillana, bhajan) and the closing mangalam.
- **Hindustani vocal music**: dhrupad (nom-tom alap, then compositions in four parts, sthayi, antara, sanchari and abhog, on pakhawaj; dhamar in 14 beats for Holi songs); khayal (bada khayal in a slow tala, then a chhota khayal in a fast one, with alap, bol-alap, bol-taan and taan); tarana; tappa (fast, intricate, from the songs of Punjabi camel drivers); thumri, dadra, kajri, chaiti and hori (light-classical, romantic and seasonal); ghazal; bhajan.
- **Hindustani instrumental music**: alap, then jor and jhala, then the gat. The Masitkhani gat is slow and the Razakhani gat is fast. Jugalbandi is a duet of two soloists.
- **Dance music**: Bharatanatyam's margam (alarippu, jatiswaram, sabdam, varnam, padam, tillana), Kathak (tatkar footwork and bol recitation), Odissi, Kuchipudi, Mohiniyattam and Manipuri, each with its own repertoire and percussion.

## 7. Instruments (organology)

- **The Natyashastra's four classes** (the same idea as the later Western Sachs-Hornbostel system):
  - **Tata vadya** (strings): the veena family, sitar, sarod, sarangi, violin, tanpura, santoor, swarmandal, ektara.
  - **Sushira vadya** (winds): bansuri and venu, shehnai, nadaswaram, harmonium (a free-reed import, now standard), shankha (conch), the kombu and ransingha horns.
  - **Avanaddha vadya** (membranes): tabla, pakhawaj, mridangam, dholak, dhol, thavil, chenda, kanjira, damaru, edakka.
  - **Ghana vadya** (solids): ghatam, manjira and talam cymbals, kartal and khartal, the jal tarang, morsing, bells, gongs.
- **A graduate also knows**: the tuning of the sitar (playing strings, chikari drone strings and the taraf sympathetic strings), the tabla's dayan and bayan with the syahi black spot, the mridangam's tuning, the veena's frets set in wax, the violin's adaptation to the Carnatic seated position, and the basic care of each instrument.
- **Orchestration in Indian film and fusion**: Indian leads (bansuri, shehnai, sitar, sarangi, veena, nadaswaram), the Western string section, brass, guitars, keyboards and synths, and the percussion layer (tabla or dholak plus a kit or programmed drums).

## 8. Aesthetics (rasa and bhava)

- **Rasa theory**: Bharata's formula is that rasa is born from the union of vibhava (the causes), anubhava (the outward signs) and vyabhicharibhava (passing feelings). There are 8 sthayibhavas (lasting emotions) that become the 8 rasas; shanta is the 9th, defended by Abhinavagupta (10th to 11th century), who explained rasa as a universal, impersonal delight the listener tastes. The rasa table is in music.md §3.
- **Raga and bhava**: a raga "colours the mind" (ranjayati iti ragah). Its mood comes from its notes, its phrases and its ornaments, and from the time it is played.
- **Ragamala painting**: the 16th to 19th-century paintings that show ragas and raginis as people and scenes. It is a strong visual source for Shorts.
- **Aesthetic values**: sukha bhava (the pleasing quality), manodharma (creative improvisation within the rules), and the balance between the tradition (sampradaya) and the individual voice.

## 9. Western music theory (the core of any BMus)

- **Intervals**: from the unison to the octave, perfect, major, minor, augmented and diminished. Consonance and dissonance. The harmonic series (it explains why Sa-Pa and Sa-Ma are so stable, and the tanpura's sound).
- **Scales and modes**: major, the three minor forms, the 7 church modes (Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian), pentatonic, whole-tone, chromatic, blues. Their matching melakartas and thaats are in music.md §14.
- **Chords and harmony**: triads (major, minor, diminished, augmented), seventh chords, inversions, Roman numeral analysis, functional harmony (tonic, subdominant and dominant), cadences (perfect, plagal, imperfect, interrupted), modulation, secondary dominants, borrowed chords. Film tricks: the minor-iv chord for nostalgia, a pedal point for suspense, the "epic" i-VI-III-VII, and chromatic mediants for wonder.
- **Voice leading and counterpoint**: Fux's five species, no parallel 5ths or octaves, contrary motion, and Bach chorales as the model. Indian music is melodic and drone-based, so harmonising a raga is a fusion choice: keep the drone, avoid chords that clash with the raga's notes, and use open 5ths and modal harmony.
- **Rhythm and meter**: simple and compound meter (4/4 against 6/8), syncopation, polyrhythm (3 against 2), additive meters (7/8 = 3+2+2, which is close to Rupak and Misra Chapu).
- **Form**: binary, ternary, rondo, theme and variations, sonata form, verse-chorus. The parallels in Indian music: sthayi and antara, pallavi, anupallavi and charanam.
- **Orchestration**: the families (strings, woodwinds, brass, percussion, keys), instrument ranges, doubling, texture (monophony, homophony, polyphony, heterophony; Indian ensembles are often heterophonic).
- **Music history**: Medieval, Renaissance, Baroque, Classical, Romantic, the 20th century, minimalism (Philip Glass and Steve Reich, both influenced by Indian music), and film-score history (leitmotif from Wagner to modern film).

## 10. Applied and practical skills a graduate has

- Singing or playing the basic exercises in all the main ragas, and recognising ragas by ear from their pakad (Mr. Raga cannot listen, so he recognises them from notes and descriptions).
- Keeping tala with hand counts while singing (Carnatic) or reciting a theka (Hindustani).
- Writing a raga's arohana and avarohana, vadi and samvadi, pakad, time and rasa, and comparing similar ragas (Yaman with Bhupali, Darbari with Adana, Todi with Bhairavi, Kalyani with Shankarabharanam).
- Composing simple pieces (a sargam geet, a lakshan geet or a geetham) and transcribing a performance.
- Accompaniment basics: tanpura tuning, harmonium, simple tabla thekas.
- Music in society: copyright for compositions against traditional repertoire, recording-industry history, music teaching and therapy, and the ethics of sampling and of AI music (no imitation of living artists, credit to sources).

## 11. Classic comparisons (common exam and Short material)

- **Yaman and Bhupali**: both are evening Kalyan-thaat ragas; Bhupali is pentatonic (no Ma, no Ni) and Yaman uses the tivra Ma.
- **Bhairav and Kalingada**: they share the same notes, but Bhairav is grave with an andolan on komal Re and Dha, while Kalingada is lighter.
- **Darbari and Adana**: both are Asavari Kanada ragas; Darbari is slow and grave in the low register, Adana is fast in the upper register.
- **Mohanam and Bhupali**: the same pentatonic notes in the two systems, with a different feel.
- **Kirwani and Keeravani**: the same scale (harmonic minor), borrowed from Carnatic into Hindustani.
- **Kafi and Kharaharapriya**: Dorian in both systems.
- **Hindustani and Carnatic**: their differences in improvisation, gamaka, concert structure, percussion, the place of composition, and language (Hindi, Braj and Urdu against Telugu, Sanskrit and Tamil).
