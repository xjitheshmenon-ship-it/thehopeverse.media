# Mr. Raga's film-scoring handbook

How the great film composers score pictures, reduced to what a 30-90 second Short needs. Mr. Raga uses it for every music brief, together with music.md. As everywhere, composers and films are named here only to teach; **never put a name, film or theme in a music prompt**, only the traits.

## 1. The core craft

- **Underscore and source music**: underscore is the score only the audience hears. Source (diegetic) music plays inside the scene, from a radio, a wedding band or a temple. Our Shorts are almost always underscore. For a Kissa or Uno Reverse scene at a wedding or party, describe it as "source-like": a band in the distance, with filtered and roomy sound.
- **Spotting**: choose where music starts, changes and stops. Rule: music enters on a change (a new idea, a reveal, an emotion) and never in the middle of a sentence. In a Short the spots are the hook, the turn and the payoff.
- **The arc of tension and release**: build with a rising pitch, a growing number of layers, rising tempo or density, a held pedal note and unresolved harmony. Release with a resolution to the tonic, a drop in density, a warm major chord or a return to the opening theme. Every Short should have exactly one big release, in the payoff.
- **Leitmotif**: a short theme (2-6 notes or a fixed instrument and texture) tied to a character, place or idea. It was developed by Wagner and made the language of Hollywood by John Williams. In Indian cinema the hero's theme is the strongest example. For our recurring series, give each lead a fixed lead instrument and a mode (e.g. Nima = bansuri in the Pahari mode; the husband in Uno Reverse = clarinet; the wife = sitar) and keep them across episodes.
- **Hit points and sync**: a film composer lands musical accents on cuts or actions. Our music is generated, not synced frame by frame, so ask for the accents as timings in seconds ("a soft stinger at about 12 s, a lift at about 40 s") and keep a steady pulse so the edits feel locked to the music.
- **Stingers**: a short, sharp accent for a shock or a punchline (a brass stab, a tabla tihai, a reversed cymbal into a hit, a piano cluster).
- **Silence and space**: the most powerful tool, but dangerous in Shorts, where silence loses viewers. Use "drop to a single held drone" instead of real silence.
- **Mickey-mousing**: music that copies every action (classic cartoons). It is good for comedy and children's content, and cheap-sounding in drama, news and nature.
- **Ostinato and pedal**: a repeating pattern over a held note. It is the engine of modern thriller and action scores, and it sits cleanly under a voice.
- **Temp track trap**: never ask for a copy of a known score's sound and structure. Describe the function ("tension that rises in 4-bar steps"), not the reference.
- **Orchestration for emotion**: solo instruments are intimate (one cello, one bansuri); strings in unison are yearning; the low brass is power and threat; the high strings in tremolo are anxiety; the celesta and glockenspiel are wonder and childhood; the solo piano is reflection; the choir is the sacred or epic (we use pads, not voices).
- **Harmony for emotion**: major is open and hopeful; minor is sad or serious; the Lydian mode is magic and wonder (the raised 4th, the same as Kalyani and Yaman); Dorian is noble melancholy; Phrygian is menace and exotic danger; the minor-iv chord is nostalgia; chromatic mediants (C major to A-flat major) are awe and the epic; tritones and clusters are horror and suspense.

## 2. Genre conventions

| Genre | Tempo and pulse | Instruments and texture | Harmony and tricks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Thriller and suspense | 70-100 BPM, a heartbeat or clock-like ostinato | low synth pulse, ticking hi-hat, string ostinato, dark pads | minor, a pedal note, rising half steps, a stinger on the reveal |
| Action | 120-150 BPM, driving | big drums, taiko or dhol, staccato strings, brass, distorted guitar | minor, ostinato riffs, risers, a drop, then a triumphant major lift |
| Romance | 60-90 BPM, gentle | piano, strings, solo violin or sitar, soft guitar, bansuri | major with sus and add9 chords, a slow melody, a warm swell at the kiss or the reunion |
| Drama and emotion | 60-80 BPM, rubato feel | solo cello, piano, strings, sarangi, shehnai | minor to major, a slow build to one big emotional swell |
| Horror | free or very slow | drones, bowed metal, prepared piano, whispers (avoid), low rumble | clusters, tritones, sudden silence, then a stinger |
| Comedy | 100-130 BPM, bouncy | pizzicato, woodwinds (bassoon, clarinet), xylophone, dholak, tuba | major, mickey-mousing, stingers on jokes, and the "uh-oh" descending trombone |
| Documentary and nature | 80-110 BPM, steady | marimba, strings, piano, soft synth, regional instruments | modal and open; wonder on reveals, tension in hunts |
| News and explainer | 90-110 BPM | soft pulse, piano, pads, light percussion | neutral and curious, never strongly emotional, a small lift at the end |
| Epic and history | 70-100 BPM, heavy | full orchestra, big drums, low brass, ancient instruments (duduk, shehnai, pakhawaj) | modal, chromatic mediants, slow builds to a massive arrival |
| Sci-fi and tech | 90-120 BPM | analogue synth arpeggios, pads, processed piano, a pulse | Lydian or Vachaspati, wide stereo, a shimmering top end |
| Mystery and wonder | 70-90 BPM | celesta, harp, music box, santoor, high strings | Lydian and whole-tone colours, questions that resolve late |
| Slice of life | 80-100 BPM, light | acoustic guitar, ukulele, piano, light percussion, bansuri | major, simple, warm, little drama |

## 3. Scoring styles of world cinema (traits to borrow; never names in prompts)

- **Classic Hollywood (1930s-1960s)**: Max Steiner and Erich Korngold brought late-Romantic opera to film. Full orchestra, wall-to-wall music, leitmotifs, lush strings, brass fanfares.
- **The modern blockbuster (1977 onwards)**: John Williams's sound, with a heroic brass theme, a full orchestra and very clear leitmotifs. Hans Zimmer and Remote Control: a hybrid orchestra plus synths, huge low-brass "braams", ostinato strings, drum loops, slow, simple chord moves that grow, and the "ticking" clock.
- **Minimalist and ambient (from the 1990s on)**: Philip Glass-style repeating patterns; drone and texture scores (Jóhann Jóhannsson, Hildur Guðnadóttir: a cello and processed sound, very sparse); the Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross electronic dread.
- **Western (Ennio Morricone)**: whistling, a twangy electric guitar, the jaw harp, solo trumpet, a choir and very long build-ups. It became the language of every standoff.
- **European art cinema**: intimate chamber music, solo piano, accordion (French), the waltz, and silence used boldly. Nino Rota's circus-like melancholy (Italy); Yann Tiersen's piano and accordion toy sound (France); Zbigniew Preisner's sacred stillness (Poland).
- **Japan**: Joe Hisaishi's lyrical piano and strings, a simple pentatonic melody and a nature-pastoral feel (the Studio Ghibli sound); taiko and shakuhachi for period dramas; anime's mixes of orchestra, rock and electronic music; and the idea of ma (meaningful space).
- **Korea**: K-drama scoring has a piano-and-strings melodrama with an emotional "OST" ballad theme, thriller scores built on tight string ostinatos, and gugak colours (haegeum, gayageum) in historical dramas.
- **China and Hong Kong**: wuxia scores (erhu, pipa, guzheng, dizi and huge orchestras), the Tan Dun cello-and-Chinese-instrument fusion, and Hong Kong action's synth-and-percussion drive.
- **Iran**: very sparse, often only source music (Kiarostami-style realism), with the santur, ney and kamancheh in historical stories.
- **Bollywood background scores**: song-led (the BGM often quotes the film's songs; we never do), loud emotional cues, the shehnai for weddings, the sarangi for grief, the sitar for romance, a thunderous percussion and brass "entry" for the hero, a sitar or synth "dhinchak" sting on a joke, and the full-orchestral "maa" (mother) theme.
- **South Indian scores (Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada)**: Ilaiyaraaja's thematic writing (pastoral flute and violin themes, a bass-guitar counter-line); A. R. Rahman's atmospheric pads and world-music rhythms; Anirudh's "mass" builds (an ostinato, a riser, one silent beat, then a drop); M. M. Keeravani's epic orchestra-and-chant (we leave out the chant); and the Malayalam new-wave realism (sparse, folk-based, guitar or a single instrument). The full composer profiles are in music.md §13.

## 4. Sound design meets music

- In modern films, the line between score and sound design is thin. Risers (a rising noise or a reversed cymbal), "whooshes", sub-bass drops, "braams", ticking clocks, heartbeats, drones and impacts are all part of the BGM.
- For our Shorts, ask the music AI for musical versions of these: "a riser into the drop", "a soft sub-bass impact on the reveal", "a ticking clock-like hi-hat pulse". Keep them few (1-2 per Short), and never louder than the voice.
- Ambience (rain, a market, wind) belongs to video.py's footage and voice track, not to the music prompt; ask only for "airy, roomy" space.

## 5. Writing a timed cue brief for a Short (what Mr. Raga writes)

A Short has one music track, but it can carry 3-4 cues inside it. Write the prompt with timed sections in plain words (ElevenLabs and Lyria both follow them). Shape:
"[genre and tradition], [lead instruments], [raga or mode], [BPM]. 0-5 s: [hook cue]. 5-25 s: [build cue]. 25-40 s: [turn cue]. 40-55 s: [payoff cue and resolve]. [mix notes]. Instrumental only, no vocals, no singing, no chanting, no spoken words."

Rules:
- Match the cue times to the script: count about 2.5 spoken words a second to find where the turn and the payoff fall.
- Only one big lift, at the payoff.
- Keep the same lead instrument and key through all the cues, so it sounds like one piece and not a medley.
- End on a held, resolved note (it loops cleanly if the track is short).

Example (a Sankalp Short about a space launch, about 50 s):
"Modern Indian cinematic pride score, shehnai lead over strings, deep drums and a soft synth pulse, in the mood of Raga Desh, 100 BPM. 0-5 s: a single shehnai phrase over a low tanpura drone. 5-25 s: strings and a soft dhol pulse enter, building anticipation. 25-40 s: a rising riser and a tense string ostinato for the countdown. 40-50 s: a big triumphant lift with brass and full drums, then a warm held resolve. Wide cinematic mix, sparse midrange under the narration. Instrumental only, no vocals, no singing, no chanting, no spoken words."

## 6. Scene scoring for our series

- **Kissa (Hindi micro drama, paused)**: a K-drama structure scored in Hindi: piano-and-strings melodrama with a sarangi or bansuri lead, one theme per couple, a tension ostinato for confrontations, and a cliffhanger cue that rises and stops on an unresolved chord (it is the episode's last second).
- **Uno Reverse (Hinglish couple comedy)**: comedy scoring with mickey-mousing allowed. Bouncy dholak and pizzicato for the setup; a clarinet "mischief" motif for the prank; a stinger (a brass toot or a tabla tihai) on the reveal of the role swap; and a warm sitar-and-strings resolve for the making-up ending. Fixed leitmotifs: husband = clarinet, wife = sitar.
- **Nima (Himalayan slice-of-life)**: the Ghibli-like pastoral approach with Himalayan colours. A bansuri theme (her leitmotif) in the Pahari mode, santoor shimmer, a soft hand drum in a Kullu nati lilt, and light, unhurried cues. Small surprises get celesta or bells, not stingers; festivals get distant dhol-damau and ransingha.
- **Instinct (nature)**: the documentary approach. Wonder cues for behaviour reveals; tension ostinatos (low drums, string spiccato) for hunts, which release into calm when the animal escapes or succeeds; never horror scoring for animals.
- **Hopeverse, Sankalp and Sawaal (news)**: the explainer approach. Neutral curiosity, one lift at the payoff, and no strong emotional manipulation in news; sombre and plain for tragedy.

## 7. Common mistakes to avoid

- Music that is "about" the topic but not the emotion (a sitar for every India story).
- Too many cues in a 45-second Short (it sounds like a medley).
- A big drop under an important sentence.
- A melody that competes with the narration.
- The same raga, tempo and instruments on every Short of a channel (vary them; keep only the channel's signature lead).
- Horror or thriller clichés under real tragedies.
- Copying a famous theme's rhythm and melody shape (a copyright risk, even without the name).

What the six share, and what our takes missed:
- **Slow, heavy pulse.** 83-117 BPM, felt in half-time (42-59). Swagger, not speed. Our early takes at 128-140 BPM were too fast.
- **Built from sustained electronic sound, not drums.** Only 16-28% of the energy is percussive, and a held sub or synth bass sounds in 36-67% of frames. Ask for "distorted synth bass", "layered analogue and digital synths", "processed and resampled hits", "unique electronic sound design" ahead of any live instrument.
- **Dark.** Only 2-5% of the energy is above 2 kHz (centroid 1.5-2.3 kHz). No bright hi-hats or shimmering pads. Ask for "dark, low-mid heavy, no bright hi-hats".

Rules for the next briefs (on top of section 8):
1. **Cinematic body first:** a full, steady, tonal low-mid layer from start to finish (brass section, low strings, a male choir pad or a veena), never empty. Synths add colour and weight; they don't replace this layer.
2. **No pumping:** ask for "steady sustained bass, no side-chain pumping, no EDM drops, no on-off bass". Energy changes only at 4-8 second phrase points, by adding layers (more top end, more percussion), never by switching layers off.
3. **A real theme:** a 2-4 bar (8-16 beat) melodic theme that returns clearly, not a 1-2 beat loop.
4. **Groove with space:** syncopated accents with small gaps before the beat, not even 16th-note hats.
5. **Clean tones, not noise:** "clear, tonal, punchy; no white-noise sweeps, no distorted noise layers, no harsh fuzz".
6. **Always one Carnatic instrument, never the lead** (Jithesh, 28 Oct): mridangam, veena or flute (venu) as the second or third layer, answering the theme and adding fast ornamented gamaka fills. Electronic sounds lead: a synth lead carries the theme and a synth bass drives the track.
7. **Hero-introduction shape** (Mr. Raga decides structure; Jithesh judges by ear): 0-12 s tease (hero unseen: low drone, ticking pulse, rising chants, theme only hinted), 12-14 s reveal (one huge hit, a split-second hold, then the full hook), 14-40 s swagger walk (theme every 4 bars, chants answering), 40-52 s mass moment (everything), 52-60 s punch (stinger, hard final hit). The tease is quieter by design but never empty: the pulse and drone run throughout.

### 8e. Study: Tamil and Telugu film dance numbers, and how we make one fresh (29 Oct 2026)

Jithesh asked for a Tamil film dance track: fusion, a fresh sound, but keeping the style. This study is from knowledge of the genre (no clips measured yet; ask him for phone recordings to add numbers).

The style we must keep (what makes a listener say "kuthu" or "mass song" in two seconds):
- **Tamil kuthu**: the thappu/parai groove in a triplet swing (tisra nadai, the "dappan" lilt), about 95-105 BPM in the swung beat. Big open drum, low and loud, never a thin loop.
- **Telugu mass**: the dappu drum and the three-stroke "teen maar" pattern, straighter and faster (about 120-140 BPM), a wedding-band brass and shehnai/nadaswaram hook, and group shouts.
- **Shared parts**: a hook riff on a reed (nadaswaram/shehnai) or a chant in the first 5 s; call-and-response group "hey" shouts; a "hook step" section where the groove locks and the whole crowd dances one move; a drum-only interlude (the drummers' showdown, speeding up); a final hook louder than the first; an ending on one unison hit and a stop.

What is already everywhere and sounds old (avoid it):
- The EDM drop over a kuthu beat, trap hi-hats, side-chain pumping, auto-tuned vocals.
- The same loop of nadaswaram sample, the same brass stab on every beat, the stadium whistle.
- Endless fast English hook lines over a stock 4-on-the-floor kick.

Our fresh version ("Broken Heritage kuthu"), keeping the style but changing the sounds:
1. The real groove stays: thappu/parai triplet swing as the spine, played big and live.
2. The sounds change: the thavil is pitched down into the bass, the nadaswaram is chopped and bent into the lead synth (rule 9.1), ghatam and kanjira are the glitchy top percussion.
3. The chant hook is konnakol vocables (ta ka dhi mi, ta ka jhu nu), sung as a crowd chant, not lyrics. Nobody has made a kuthu hook out of konnakol.
4. The surprise (rule 9.7) is a Carnatic nadai change: the hook step switches from triplet swing to a straight four for one section, then snaps back. It is a composed rhythm change, not a DJ trick (rule 9.10).
5. The drummers' showdown ends on a korvai: a phrase played three times that lands exactly on beat one.
6. Identity: the 3-note motif in the first 5 s and the last hit; one reversed veena swell into the main hook.

- **R23** (34.5 s, ~117 BPM): sections change at about 8, 12 and 26 s. Each change is set up by a 2-4 s strip-down: the bass drops out (sub share 0.29 to 0.01-0.08), only the high layer plays (-7 to -8 dB), then the full mix comes back bigger (-13.7 dB, louder than the opening -16 dB).
- **R24** (24.4 s, ~96 BPM): 0-4 s one high instrument alone (no bass, 57-63% above 2 kHz); about 4 s a second layer joins (+3 dB); at 6 s the full mix lands with the bass (+8 dB, sub 0.03 to 0.27). Dip again at 14 s for 2 s.
Pattern: a full section change every 8-18 s; the start builds one layer at a time; the bass leaving for 2-4 s (played, not muted) is what makes each change feel big.

## 9. Our sound identity: "Broken Heritage + sonic logo" (Jithesh chose it, 28 Oct 2026)

Jithesh: "everything is good, but there is no uniqueness; we need our own identity." Identity = fixed signatures in every track, so a listener knows it is ours in 3 seconds. Every brief must carry all five:
1. **Broken Heritage, the electronic layer is made of Carnatic sound:** veena, mridangam, nadaswaram and venu are chopped, stuttered, reversed, pitched and time-stretched into the electronic parts. A stuttering, glitch-chopped veena is the synth lead's partner; a mridangam pitched down and stretched becomes the deep bass hit; nadaswaram phrases are reversed and pitch-shifted into risers; ghatam and kanjira are cut into tight glitchy percussion.
2. **The Hopeverse motif (sonic logo):** a rising 3-note call (root, up a minor third, up to the fifth, the last note held and bent upward), in the first 5 seconds and at the end of every track, on any lead sound.
3. **Signature sound:** a reversed veena swell into a hit, used ONCE per track (the main reveal), not at every section change (Jithesh, 29 Oct: the same trick everywhere sounds like a DJ mix).
4. **Kept from what Jithesh liked:** electronic lead, cinematic brass and strings body, wordless crowd chants, clean tones, no pumping, no long gaps; the Carnatic instruments are never a clean "live" lead, they appear processed.
5. **Not used:** clean unprocessed Carnatic solos as the lead, generic EDM drops.
6. **Ear-to-ear travel is a rare signature moment, not a constant effect** (Jithesh, 28 Oct): use it only 1-2 times per track, and give each moment its own travel rhythm, never the same steady sweep twice. The first demo with only one or two moments beat the constant auto-pan version. Default pair: one slow glide from left to right in the tease, and one ping-pong that speeds up into the final hit. Bass and kick stay centred. Apply it after generation with /mnt/project-files/music/tools/moments.py (set the windows to the track's sections), and check it with tools/pan.py. The rest of the track stays at the natural reference travel (about 0.1-0.35 per s, see 8d).
7. **Micro touches and a surprise (tiny one-off details as identity)** (Jithesh, 28 Oct): every track MUST have at least one surprise the listener does not expect (a sudden drop to near silence, an odd-beat hit, a stutter, a key lift or a motif in an unexpected place), and each track uses a different one so it feels like our signature. Also put 2-4 small changes at section changes, each used only once and each a different kind. For example: the top drains away for one beat before a hit, a 1/16-note stutter before a drop, a tiny hook variation on the last repeat. Ask for these in the prompt as played musical moments (see rule 10); don't add them with micro.py any more.
8. **Keep the grip in the middle** (Jithesh, 28 Oct: "from 35 to 45 seconds we lost that grip"): measured, the hits fell from 13 to 9 per 2 s while the sound only grew brighter, so nothing new happened for about 18 s. Rule: never go more than 8 s without a new event. Around 60% of the track, bring back the main hook with full drums plus a chant call, and keep the hit rate at least at the walk section's level until the punch.
9. **Our own sounds, not stock sounds** (Jithesh, 29 Oct: "uniqueness in music and rhythm, but not in the sounds used"): every track gets a signature sound palette made from the subject's own real-world sounds turned into instruments, on top of Broken Heritage. A dog: a bark pitched down into the bass hit, collar-bell jingle as the hi-hat, claw taps as the shaker, a squeaky-toy squeak pitched into the lead stab, panting as a breathy pulse. A hero in a city: footsteps, a lighter flick, engine rev as risers. Name these sounds in the prompt as the instruments. No muting or dropping one ear in post: Jithesh heard the 0.2 s ear blink as a gap.
10. **Transitions are composed, like a musician, not DJ tricks** (Jithesh, 29 Oct: "transition parts are not interesting, it sounds like a DJ mix, not a musician's art"). Every section change is a different, played musical idea: a melodic pickup fill that a real instrument plays into the next section; a key lift or a borrowed chord; one instrument left alone for a bar (a lonely flute or veena phrase) before the band answers; strings or brass building a counter-melody that becomes the next theme; a drum fill a real drummer would play; a shift to half-time feel. The main melody should develop (vary, answer, climb) instead of looping. No risers or white-noise sweeps between every section, no filter sweeps, no build-and-drop, no stutters, tape stops or hard mutes added in post: those are DJ devices. Post work is limited to mastering and the rare ear-travel moments (rule 6); the surprise (rule 7) must be composed into the music itself.

11. **Transitions and layered starts** (Jithesh, 29 Oct, refs R23-R24, §8f; tightened 10:17: "every 8-12 sec there should be a transition with a proper hook"): a complete section change every 8-12 s, and EVERY transition lands on a proper hook (the signature riff or a clear variation of it, played by a new instrument or in a new way); no breaks or quiet stretches longer than 4 s (new groove, new lead or new texture, not just louder). Start with ONE instrument alone, add a second around 4 s, the full mix with the bass around 6-8 s. Before a big section, let the bass and drums step out for 2-4 s while one high layer keeps playing (composed by the players, never a mute or a filter, rule 10), then bring everything back bigger than before.
12. **Balance and a mysterious start, for EVERY track (dance or BGM)** (Jithesh, 29 Oct on the dance v4: "a dumping of all the electronic noises, that's not music"; "it always makes a lot of instruments in starting"):
   - **Balance:** real acoustic instruments (Carnatic ones such as veena, mridangam, nadaswaram, flute, thavil, plus strings, brass and drums) carry about half the music, and fresh electronic sounds carry the other half. Each is heard clearly; the electronics answer and colour the real instruments, never bury them. No wall of synths.
   - **Mystery first:** open with only ONE or TWO sounds (for example a lone veena phrase over a soft low drone), hold that mystery for 6-10 s, then add one layer at a time and build steadily "like a highway" to the full mix by about 20-25 s. Never start with many instruments at once. This extends rule 11.
13. **The wow factor** (Jithesh, 29 Oct on the dance v5: sounds, instruments and style right, "a real Tamil movie BGM", but "missing the wow factor, the attention-making factor"). Every track needs, on top of rules 11-12:
   - **An earworm signature riff:** one short, catchy 4-6 note riff, stated clearly at the first groove and repeated (with small variations) so the listener hums it after one play.
   - **An attention grab in the first 3 s**, even inside the mystery start: one striking sound (a deep bent note with a cinematic low boom, a sharp nadaswaram cry), not a polite intro.
   - **At least two composed wow moments:** a full one-beat band silence and then a massive slam when the groove lands; a call-and-response duel between a real instrument and a synth that gets faster; double-time drum rolls into the hook; the last hook lifted up a whole step (key change) and played biggest. Each moment is played by the band (rule 10), never a post trick.
14. **The senior hit-composer's touch** (Jithesh, 29 Oct on the dance v6: "it has that wow factor", but it doesn't sound like a senior composer with many blockbuster hits; "that musician touch is not there"). The craft that separates a master's track from a good one (traits from music.md §13, never names or tunes):
   - **A real melody, not a riff loop:** a singable tune built from question-and-answer phrases, rooted in a raga with Carnatic gamakam slides, that develops (varies, climbs, answers itself) through the track; the earworm riff (rule 13) is part of it.
   - **Counterpoint:** a counter-melody in strings or flute answering the lead, and a melodic bass line that moves like a second melody instead of only following the kick.
   - **Real harmony:** a moving chord progression with one borrowed chord or a pedal bass under changing chords, not one static chord.
   - **An interlude showpiece:** a short virtuoso passage (a flute-and-violin counterpoint, or a nadaswaram solo with gamakam over a held groove) like the interludes of hit film songs.
   - **Economy and polish:** each instrument has one clear job, space between phrases, a human groove feel, a glossy, wide, international-sounding mix with deep sub and airy reverb.
15. **The Indian touch is the STYLE, not the instruments** (Jithesh, 29 Oct on Monsoon Duel v2: "it's all same, Western music kind of thing; I need that Indian touch, especially South Indian movie music"; then: "Indian touch doesn't mean you have to use these instruments; it means the style of music"). Any instruments are fine (synths, guitars, strings, brass); what must be Indian is how the music is written and played:
   - **Melody:** built on a raga, sung-style phrasing with gamakam slides, bends and grace notes played on whatever lead plays it; long held notes that bend, short repeated-note runs, a tune you could sing, not an arpeggio or a power-chord riff.
   - **Harmony:** modal, sitting on one drone or pedal bass with few chord changes, the melody carrying the emotion; no Western trailer chord climbs (i-VI-III-VII loops) or big Western cadences.
   - **Rhythm:** South Indian film grooves (the Tamil mass-BGM bounce, the dappankuthu/teen-maar off-beat, a swinging 6/8 folk feel, tight rhythmic unison stabs with the melody), not a straight Western rock or trailer pulse.
   - **Structure:** the mass-BGM way: the hero theme stated as a whistle-able hook, answered call-and-response, built up, one beat of silence, then the hook slams back bigger; chorus shouts on the hook's accents.

16. **Real instruments keep their natural sound** (Jithesh, 29 Oct on First Love v7: the tumbi, flute and others were "not used in a natural way"; "a lot of echo, which destroys the natural thing, the originality", plus "DJ work" and pauses that break the natural rhythm. 19:06: this rule is about THOSE INSTRUMENTS, not a rule for romance or any one track type). In every track, any real instrument named as itself (flute, tumbi, violin, santoor, tabla, veena played as a veena) must sound exactly like that instrument played by a real musician: close and natural, no heavy echo or delay on it, not chopped or glitched, and its rhythm flowing without DJ-style breaks. The processed Broken Heritage layer (rule 1) stays a separate electronic layer and never replaces the natural instrument.
