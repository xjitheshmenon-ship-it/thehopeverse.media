## 2. The honest caveat: what scientists would say

I found **no scientist's comment on this specific event**. Do not invent one. The caveats below come from general knowledge of belugas and from the studies I checked.

- **Captivity and training.** Mila is a trained aquarium beluga who works with divers all the time. Pushing or mouthing a person is not clear proof of understanding "danger" or of intent to rescue. It could be a learned behaviour, curiosity, play, or a response to a body moving oddly. This is my inference, not a quote from the sources.
- **Honest verdict.** What is documented is that a beluga approached a struggling diver and she reached the surface. "Rescue" is the press framing and the organizer's opinion.

**Study 1, on social behaviour:** O'Corry-Crowe et al., "Group structure and kinship in beluga whale societies", *Scientific Reports*, 2020, from Florida Atlantic University's Harbor Branch Institute. [Open-access text on PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7351962/).
- It covered 10 locations across the Arctic.
- Belugas often interact with close kin, but also frequently associate with distant relatives and unrelated individuals.
- The authors compare this to some human societies, unlike killer and pilot whales.
- This supports "belugas are highly social", but it does not show altruism towards humans.

**Study 2, optional, on mimicry:** Ridgway et al., *Current Biology*, 23 Oct 2012. A male beluga named Noc imitated human speech. It shows belugas can attend closely to humans and match their sounds. [LiveScience](https://www.livescience.com/24166-beluga-whale-mimics-human-voice.html) covers it. It also shows captive belugas' behaviour towards people is a special case.

**Human comparison:** a trained lifeguard dog or a pool lifeguard. A lifeguard acts on training and a reflex to someone in trouble, and we still call it a rescue. Mila's case is similar, but we cannot know what she "felt". Alternative: like some human societies, belugas associate with many non-relatives, which fits a species that approaches strangers.
