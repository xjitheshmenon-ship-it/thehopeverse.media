## Caveats from later research (verified)

- **Active teaching is rare.** NPR describes the 1991 paper as famous for its exceedingly rare report of two cases of active teaching (Barbara J. King, 2016). The CogWeb summary adds that other observers with hundreds of person-years in the field report active teaching even less often.
- **Other sites found none.** Moore reports that Lonsdorf (Gombe, termite fishing) and Matsuzawa et al. (Bossou) saw no teaching. At Bossou, mothers were tolerant of watching juveniles but did not teach.
- **Most learning is by watching.** Matsuzawa and others use the "master–apprentice" model from sushi making: years of silent but tolerated observation.
- **The debate continues.** Discover Wildlife says the question of whether apes can teach remains disputed. Boesch accepts there is some scepticism but stays convinced the Taï chimpanzees learn this way.
- **Newer evidence from another site.** Musgrave et al. (2016, *Scientific Reports*, Goualougo Triangle, Congo) used camera-trap video. It reports that tool transfers during termite fishing meet the scientific criteria for teaching. NPR dates that coverage to 20 Oct 2016.

## Human comparison

Boesch et al. (2019) compared Taï chimpanzees with Mbendjele children in the Republic of Congo cracking the same Panda nuts. Both species learn slowly, with similar mistakes, and need years of practice. The chimpanzees reached adult efficiency earlier than the humans did. Adult efficiency did not differ significantly between the two species.

- **Expert help:** it came about every 10 minutes for chimpanzees and every 20 minutes for the children.
- **Fair framing:** "a parent guiding a child's hands" is a reasonable analogy. It is an analogy, not a measured finding. Avoid saying the chimpanzee "taught like a human".
