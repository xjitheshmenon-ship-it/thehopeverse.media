## Honest caveats for the Short
- **Not every day:** Wikipedia says he returned "almost every day". Say "presque chaque jour". Punctuality is partly legend. The 5–6 pm return is a biographer's account, not a log.
- **He was not always loved:** he was often treated as a stray, and some vendors, station workers and children mistreated him. Some vendors did feed him. The yakitori-vendor "he came for food" claim is therefore partly plausible, but food does not explain nine years. After the 1932 article, people brought him food while he waited.
- **Before fame:** he was caught by dog catchers several times, and his collars were stolen.
- **Also not confirmed:** a claim that Ueno took him to classes is denied by a relative of the gardener.
- **Health:** he had mange in 1929, and his left ear drooped after a fight. For his last three months he struggled to walk. His final night included visits to the shops and rooms of the station.
- **Cause of death debate:** the yakitori theory ran until the 1980s, and the 2011 necropsy settled it.
- **Descendants:** many people falsely claimed to own his offspring, but only one son, Kuma-kō, is known.

## Unique fact to build the Short on
**Best candidate:** the yakitori skewers were a myth-buster. For decades people blamed the skewers in his stomach. The 2011 necropsy found cancer and heartworm, and said the skewers did not cause his death. The Wikipedia article is one source, so this is **not yet two-source confirmed**. Before publishing, check the University of Tokyo or a major outlet.

**Alternative, if the above fails:** he never waited at Shibuya for his first arrival, because he came in at Ueno Station.
