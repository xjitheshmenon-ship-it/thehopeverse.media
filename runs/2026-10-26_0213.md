## Sources and dates

- **ScienceDaily:** the Sep 15, 2026 page is a re-run. The page is dated September 15, 2026, but ScienceDaily's original release is dated May 10, 2026. So the story is about 4–5 months old, not new. Call it "a 2026 study", not "new this week".

## 1. Mechanism
- **What was detected:** TROPOMI, an instrument on ESA's Sentinel-5P satellite, saw formaldehyde (HCHO) in the plume. HCHO is a short-lived by-product of methane breakdown.
  - The paper reports an HCHO enhancement of up to 12 ppb (±10%) at 30 km altitude, persisting for ten days or more.
  - In the paper's own words, it is the highest HCHO enhancement ever recorded in the stratosphere.
  - The paper argues the HCHO must have formed inside the plume. Its midday lifetime is only 2.5 hours, yet the signal was still there 20 hours later and up to Jan 25.
- **Chlorine, not hydroxyl:** the paper rules out hydroxyl (OH) as the main driver.
  - OH would have needed at least 2,300 Gg of methane. The ACE-FTS satellite would have seen that, and it didn't.
  - Chlorine radicals need only about 330 Gg, which is realistic.
  - Bromine chemistry is also rejected. It can supply about 1.5×10⁴ Cl cm⁻³ s⁻¹ against the 3×10⁵ observed, roughly 20 times too little.
- **Where the chlorine came from:** the paper says it needs 2–5 Gg of new chlorine per day that is "unexplained by known mechanisms".
  - The proposed source is iron–chloride photochemistry on sulfate-coated volcanic ash. The ash was mixed with sea salt from the huge seawater injection, and sunlight drives the reaction.
  - So "sea salt" is only half the story. Ash iron plus sea-salt chloride plus sunlight is the full proposal.
  - The authors say it is "plausible" and that confirmation "will require dedicated modeling and laboratory studies".

## 2. How much methane, and over what period
- **Rate:** total methane oxidation was 900 ± 220 Mg/day, implying at least 330 Gg of volcanic methane was injected into the stratosphere.
  - The paper gives a midday peak of 75 ± 18 Mg per hour.
  - ScienceDaily and SciTechDaily round the injected figure to "around 300 Gg". The paper says "at least 330 Gg", so use the paper's number.
- **Period:** at least 10 days (Jan 16–25, 2022). The paper says the HCHO signal "persisted for weeks and possibly months", but the rate itself is only supported for about 10 days.
- **My own arithmetic, not stated in the paper:** 900 Mg/day × 10 days is about 9 Gg. That is roughly 3% of the 330 Gg estimate. The destruction was real but a small fraction of the methane released.
- **Major caveat:** the 330 Gg injection is not measured. It is back-calculated from the chlorine assumption.
  - The paper's other estimates (via CO₂/CH₄ ratios) span 4 to 40,000 Gg.
  - The ACE-FTS non-detection caps the injection at under 1,000 Gg.
  - Note: the paper's text says "Mg/day" in the abstract and "ton/day" in the conclusion. These are the same unit.

## 4. What it means for methane-removal ideas
- **The paper's real contribution is a measuring method.** HCHO from satellites can detect enhanced methane oxidation, even over oceans where methane sensors are weak.
- **Sensitivity check:** a modelled iron-based intervention (Meidan et al. 2024) would remove about 3.1 Gg CH₄ per hour. That is far above the 75 Mg per hour seen at Hunga Tonga, which was clearly detectable. So the method could verify a future trial.
- **Idea for Short:** the eruption is a natural proof-of-concept for how chlorine chemistry can break down methane. It is not a recipe.
- **The authors' own caveat:** the paper says open-air approaches "if proven to be climate beneficial and cost-effective" could have the largest potential. The ScienceDaily text adds that manipulating atmospheric chemistry "would require careful study" of unintended effects.
- **Rarity:** the paper says the mechanism may not matter much in other eruptions. Hunga Tonga had exceptional seawater injection and modest SO₂, which suits iron–chloride chemistry.
- **Ozone link:** the chlorine in the plume was also tied to ozone depletion there, so side effects are a real concern. This is my reading of the paper's ozone discussion, not an explicit "side effects" statement.

## 5. Researchers' own caveats (from the paper)
1. The mechanism is "plausible", not confirmed. It needs modelling and lab work.
2. The chlorine source is "unexplained by known mechanisms".
3. The injected methane is inferred and could not be directly detected. ACE-FTS saw no enhancement above its noise.
4. A previous model (Zhu et al. 2023) explained the chlorine rate about 130 times lower than this paper infers. The gap is unresolved.
5. The HCHO method is limited by local HCHO sources such as biomass burning.
6. The mechanism may be specific to this eruption.

## Verdicts

**Line 1: Correct.**
- The abstract puts the 12 ppb enhancement at 30 km. The two plume clouds sat at 30–32 km and 27–29 km.

**Line 2: Mostly correct, but "suspected" is stronger than the paper.**

- **Fix:** "Chlorine from sea salt, reacting with that ash, is one plausible explanation the study proposes."

**Line 3: Correct on the numbers. One wording fix and one unverified word.**
- Formaldehyde of "12 ppb ± 10%" and "the highest HCHO enhancement ever recorded in the stratosphere" are both in the paper's text. Formaldehyde is a short-lived intermediate with a lifetime of about 2.5 hours at midday.
- The 12 ppb is derived, not read directly. The authors computed it from a satellite column of 1.6×10¹⁵ molec/cm² and an assumed 2 km layer thickness. The paper also gives a separate ±20% uncertainty from aerosols.
- "ESA's" is **unverified**. The paper says Sentinel-5P/TROPOMI but never says ESA, so it would be from memory.
- **Fix:** "The Sentinel-5P satellite saw formaldehyde there, an estimated 12 parts per billion, the highest ever recorded in the stratosphere."

**Line 4: Number correct. The duration and dates need hedging.**
- "900 ± 220 Mg/day" is correct, and Mg is the same as tonnes.
- The paper does not say the destruction was measured for ten days. It says the rate "may have continued for at least 10 days", because the total formaldehyde did not decline.
- The Jan 16–25 range is the span over which the formaldehyde plume was tracked. The Jan 16 overpass was the first to show it. Cloud fractions were assessed up to Jan 25. Full quantification covers only some days (16, 17, 20 and 21).
- **Fix:** "A 2026 study estimates about 900 tonnes of methane destroyed a day, possibly for ten days or more."

- The paper contradicts itself on duration. The abstract says "ten days or more", but the conclusion says "weeks and possibly months". Use ten days.

**Line 5: Correct, with one gap.**
- "Inferred, not measured": the methane was never seen. Methane breakdown is inferred from formaldehyde. ACE-FTS (a satellite instrument that measures atmospheric gases) found no methane enhancement, and the paper says the amount is too small for TROPOMI to detect.
- The injected methane (at least 330 Gg) is also inferred.
- The paper says confirmation needs "dedicated modeling and laboratory studies", so it is not only labs.
- **Fix:** "Inferred, not measured, and still to be confirmed by modelling and lab studies."

**Line 6: Correct in spirit but imprecise.**
- Satellites see formaldehyde, not methane. The method tracks enhanced methane oxidation through formaldehyde. It works over oceans, where methane sensors fail.
- The paper says the method "can be" sensitive enough for hypothetical future methane-removal schemes. It is limited by other formaldehyde sources such as fires.
- **Fix:** "Satellites may be able to spot enhanced methane breakdown, by tracking formaldehyde."
