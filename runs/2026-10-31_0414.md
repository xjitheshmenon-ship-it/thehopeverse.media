## 2016 study: how it was done
- **Paper:** Spottiswoode, Begg & Begg, "Reciprocal signaling in honeyguide-human mutualism," *Science* 353:387–389, 22 July 2016, DOI 10.1126/science.aaf4885.
- **Setting:** Niassa National Reserve, northern Mozambique.
- **Interviews:** The team interviewed 20 Yao men who use the call. All said they learned it from their fathers and that it is the best way to attract a honeyguide.
- **Recordings:** Spottiswoode recorded the "brrr-hm" call from honey hunters. She also recorded the same men saying other words in Yao, and a call from another bird species.
- **Control sounds (three conditions):**
  - the "brrr-hm" call;
  - an arbitrary Yao word, reported by AAAS as the Yao for "honeyguide…honey";
  - an unrelated bird call.
- **Method:** The sounds were played back during real honey-hunting walks. Per CSMonitor, each trial was 15 minutes, with "two brilliantly sharp-eyed honey-hunters" and Spottiswoode walking in a line through the bush (https://www.csmonitor.com/Science/2016/0721/This-wild-bird-will-lead-you-to-a-beehive-if-you-know-the-password).
- **Side observation (AAAS):** With the control sounds, honeyguides sometimes lost interest and stopped guiding (https://www.aaas.org/news/science-sweet-example-human-and-wild-animal-collaboration).

## What the bird does and what it gets
- **Behaviour:** The bird chatters and flits from tree to tree, and the hunters follow (CSMonitor). The 2023 coverage describes the same thing for the Hadza: it flies to them chattering, as if to say "follow me" (NPR, 7 Dec 2023: https://www.npr.org/sections/goatsandsoda/2023/12/07/1217800692/african-honeyguide-bird-calls-honey-human-cultural-evolution).
- **Reward:** Humans open the nest and take the honey. The bird eats the newly exposed beeswax (AAAS, 2023: https://www.aaas.org/news/honeyguide-birds-learn-culturally-distinct-calls-made-honey-hunters).
- **Wax digestion:** Audubon notes it is one of few birds that can eat and digest wax (22 Aug 2016: https://www.audubon.org/magazine/meet-greater-honeyguide-bird-understands-humans).
- **Why the birds are needed:** Spottiswoode says, "The trees are tall, and the bees are small" (Audubon).
- **Sound of the call:** It is a loud trill followed by a short grunt (UCT, 23 Aug 2016: https://science.uct.ac.za/articles/2016-08-23-how-humans-and-wild-birds-collaborate-get-precious-resources-honey-and-wax). Audubon describes the trill as rolled, like a Spaniard's.

## Named people
- **Orlando Yassene:** Yao honey hunter, photographed holding a wild female honeyguide and harvesting a nest in Niassa (EurekAlert, CSMonitor).
- **Seliano Rucunua:** Yao honey hunter, photographed holding a male honeyguide caught for research (Science news, 7 Dec 2023: https://www.science.org/content/article/birds-lead-people-honey-recognize-local-calls-their-human-helpers).
- **Maogola Mdungai:** Hadza honey hunter, photographed demonstrating the Hadza call (Science news, 7 Dec 2023).
- **Keith and Colleen Begg:** co-authors, who told Spottiswoode about the Yao claim.
- **Spottiswoode quote (AAAS):** The Yao claim implied "that humans communicate to honeyguides, too." Honeyguides were already known to communicate to humans.
- **Quotes from Yassene or Rucunua:** none found. Don't invent any.

## 2023 follow-up: regional dialects
- **Paper:** Spottiswoode & Wood, "Culturally determined interspecies communication between humans and honeyguides," *Science* 382 (6675):1155–1158, DOI 10.1126/science.adh4129. Press coverage is dated 7 Dec 2023.
- **Researchers:** Claire Spottiswoode (Cape Town and Cambridge) and Brian Wood (UCLA and Max Planck, who has worked with the Hadza since 2004).
- **Design (NPR, Science news):**
  - Researchers returned to each site with the same set of recordings: Hadza whistles, Yao trills, and people saying their own names as the control.
  - In the Hadza area of Tanzania, honeyguides were more than three times more likely to cooperate with the local Hadza whistle than with the foreign Yao call.
  - In the Yao area of Mozambique, they were roughly twice as likely to respond to the local "brrr-hm" than to the foreign whistle.
- **Wording differs by outlet:** For Mozambique, Science news says "more than twice as effective as the other two sounds." UCLA says "almost twice as likely" against the Hadza whistle. Sci.news says "more than three times" for both sites, which conflicts with UCLA and Science, so I'd avoid it. Safest phrasing: "about twice as likely."
- **Conclusion:** The birds learn local signals rather than being innately drawn to them. Spottiswoode calls it "a mosaic of interspecies communication traditions" (AAAS).
- **Other 2023 figures:**
  - NPR says honeyguides appeared 82% of the time Hadza whistles were played. I couldn't confirm what this was compared against, so I'd leave it out.
  - UCLA says honeyguides raised Hadza hunters' rate of finding bee nests by 560%. The headline is cut off in my copy, so check the full release before using it (https://newsroom.ucla.edu/releases/wild-birds-lead-people-to-honey-learn-from-them).
- **Why hunters keep their calls:** Yao and Hadza hunters say they stick with the calls from their forebears because changing them lowers the odds of attracting a honeyguide.

## Honest caveats
1. **Brood parasite:** Spottiswoode says the young bird kills its foster siblings soon after hatching. She calls it "a proper Jekyll and Hyde of the bird world" (UCT).
2. **Mutual self-interest:** The relationship has payoffs on both sides. The bird gets wax and the humans get honey. It is not altruism.
3. **Tradition at risk:** Spottiswoode warns, "There is a risk that we could lose this likely ancient part of our humanity" (AAAS). Audubon says the Yao tradition is still strong in Niassa.
4. **Sample and scope:** The 2016 study is one region, with 20 interviewees. The 2023 results come from two sites.
5. **Rates:** The 33% and 66% figures are probabilities of being guided in the experiment. Don't present them as the share of birds that "understand."
