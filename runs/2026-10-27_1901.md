## Confirmed facts (source in brackets)

- **Task:** a touchscreen shows numerals in random positions. After the subject touches the first numeral, the others are masked by white squares. The subject must then touch the hidden numerals in ascending order. [Inoue & Matsuzawa 2007, Current Biology 17(23): R1004–5, via ResearchGate]
- **Exposure times:** the "limited-hold" test used 650, 430 and 210 ms. Each session had 50 trials. [same paper, Fig. 2 caption]
- **Numerals shown:** five numerals out of 1–9. Ayumu got about 79% correct at 210 ms. [Silberberg & Kearns 2009, PubMed 19115068]
- **The "9 numerals" claim is wrong.** It circulates widely. A blogger (replicatedtypo) first wrote 9 numerals, then edited the post to say the paper only describes 5. Don't say "nine".
- **Humans:** the Current Biology figure caption gives n = 9 humans. Silberberg's abstract also says "None of nine humans" approached Ayumu's accuracy. The difference was significant at 430 ms and 210 ms (p < 0.001). [same paper; PubMed]
- **Ai, his mother:** she was also tested and did not match him. Ai is the language-trained chimp. Ayumu was the best performer of the young chimps. [Current Biology; replicatedtypo]
- **Ayumu was the only one of 6 chimps to reach that level.** The chimps had trained on a version without time pressure, about 200 trials per day. [Cook & Wilson 2010, ResearchGate]
- **Pause before answering:** Ayumu could pause several seconds, for example at a noise, and still stay accurate. His average response latency was 650 ms. [Cook & Wilson 2010]

## The honest caveat (the strongest hook)

1. **Silberberg & Kearns 2009 (Animal Cognition).** Humans who practised at 210 ms matched Ayumu. Ayumu had not trained at short delays. [Wikipedia, "Cognitive tradeoff hypothesis"] The Snopes page quotes the paper as saying Ayumu's performance "does not differ" from practised humans.
2. **Cook & Wilson 2010 (Psychonomic Bulletin & Review).** They trained 2 undergraduates under conditions like Ayumu's practice, with 650 ms delays. The humans matched and even outperformed him. They also say this "does not decisively overturn" the original claim, because Ayumu needed no practice at 210 ms. [Springer PDF]
3. **Matsuzawa's "cognitive trade-off" / semantic-link idea.** Chimps keep a strong, almost eidetic visual memory. Humans gained symbolic and language skills, and the numeral meanings may burden working memory. [Evolang summary on pure.mpg.de; Wikipedia]
4. **Online replication, a number most videos miss.** Of 2,347 attempts at 9 numerals at 210 ms, 75 succeeded (3%). Humans got the first numeral right about 80% of the time. [Roberts, "The Chimp Challenge", MPG]

## Discrepancies to avoid

- Cook & Wilson say "3 university students". The original figure says n = 9. Say "des étudiants" and avoid a number, or use "9" and cite Current Biology.
- Many viral clips say a chimp has "photographic memory" or is "smarter than humans". The paper itself invokes an eidetic-like memory, but the follow-ups don't support a general superiority.

## Unverified (from memory, check before publishing)

- **Ayumu's age:** he was born 24 April 2000, so he was about 5–7 at the time. That is consistent with "juvenile" in Cook & Wilson. His exact age in the test sessions is unverified.
- **Ai:** she was born in Africa around 1976 and is the star of the Ai Project (Kyoto, from 1978). Unverified.
- **Human accuracy at 210 ms:** I believe it was roughly 40%, but I did not retrieve the figure. Don't put it on screen.
- **Is Ayumu still alive?** My memory says yes, in the Kyoto colony, but I found no dated source. Say nothing, or state "à ma connaissance" with a check at the Kyoto University Primate Research Institute site.
- **Later papers:** I found nothing newer in this search beyond 2012.
