## (1) What the study found

**Sources:** Nagaya, Seama, Fujie, Tanaka, Sugioka & Kodaira, *Communications Earth & Environment* 7, 237. Received 5 Jun 2025, accepted 17 Feb 2026, published 27 Mar 2026. It's open access: https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-026-03347-9 . ScienceDaily cuts the journal name to "Communications Earth."

- **Who did it.** Kobe University (Dept. of Planetology and KOBEC, the Kobe Ocean-Bottom Exploration Center) and JAMSTEC, Japan's marine science agency. Lead author Nagaya has since moved to Kokusai Kogyo Co. Seama is the professor quoted in the press.
- **Method.** This was a seismic refraction survey, not monitoring over time.
  - 39 seafloor seismometers were laid along one 175 km line across the volcano: 9 spaced 2.5 km apart over the caldera and 30 spaced 5 km apart outside it.
  - The research ship *Kaimei* fired an airgun array to send sound pulses through the crust.
  - The data came from the KM21-05 cruise. The name suggests 2021, but that's my guess.
- **What they saw.** A zone where seismic waves travel slowly, directly beneath the central lava dome.
- **Depth.** About 2.5–6 km below the seafloor.
- **Shape and size.** In the 2D cross-section it's roughly a trapezoid about 4 km wide at the top, 13 km at the bottom and 3.5 km tall. It's at least as wide as the inner caldera.
  - The authors get **~220 km³** by assuming the body is round around a vertical axis. That's a big assumption to rest on a single survey line.
- **How much of it is actually molten.** Estimated **3–6% melt, "limited to 10% at most."** For comparison, the paper cites 16–20% beneath Yellowstone and 14.5–18.5% beneath Toba.
  - My own arithmetic, not stated in the paper: 220 km³ × 3–6% is roughly 7–13 km³ of melt. The 7,300-year-ago eruption released about 160 km³ (DRE, or dense-rock equivalent).
  - The authors also note the real reservoir could be bigger and more molten than their estimate.
  - A 2025 preprint gave 3–8%. Use the published 3–6%.
- **Why they call it "refilling."** This is an inference, not something they measured directly.
  - The reservoir sits where the 7,300-year-old eruption's magma was stored.
  - The dome (over 32 km³, built over roughly the last 3,900 years) has a different chemistry from the ancient eruption. That points to new magma rather than leftovers.
  - From this they conclude "at least 32 km³ of different melts" have been injected, at an average rate of more than 8.2 km³ per thousand years. That's 32 ÷ 3.9, and it could have come in steadily or in bursts.
  - Nothing here measures refilling happening right now.

**What the authors themselves say about eruption risk**
- **The paper:** these re-injection processes "could be a step towards the next giant caldera eruption," and changes in seismic wave speed are "important indicators of subsequent giant caldera eruptions." It gives no timeline and no probability.
- **The press release:** scientists are "ill-equipped to make predictions." Seama says, "Our ultimate goal is to become better able to monitor the crucial indicators of future giant eruptions."
- ⚠️ **The reassuring line isn't theirs.** ScienceDaily's Oct 23 version says the replenishment "does not mean that another catastrophic eruption is imminent." That sentence isn't in Kobe's release. Credit it to ScienceDaily, not the researchers.

## (2) The Kikai-Akahoya (K-Ah) eruption, about 7,300 years ago
- **Size:** about 160 km³ DRE (range 133–183), "probably the largest eruption of the Holocene," the last ~11,700 years. The paper cites Shimizu et al. 2024 for this; the 2024 seabed survey is what raised the estimate (ScienceDaily, 22 Feb 2024).
- **VEI:** 7, "ultra-Plinian." Wikipedia gives a bulk volume of 332–457 km³ and a date of 7165–7303 cal BP. It's a secondary source.
- **Location:** a caldera about 19 km across, 50–60 km south of Kyushu.
- **Ash spread** ⚠️ (background, not checked):
  - K-Ah ash is a key time-marker layer across southwestern and central Japan, and reportedly beyond.
  - Pyroclastic flows (the Koya flow) crossed the sea to southern Kyushu.
  - Check this against Maeno & Taniguchi 2007 (J. Volcanol. Geotherm. Res. 167) and the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program (GVP) entry.
- **Effect on Jōmon people.** New study: Crema, Kuwahata, Uchiyama, Junno, Riede & Jordan, *Science Advances* 12(42), 2026, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aeh7197. ScienceDaily ran it on 18 Oct 2026: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261017230023.htm
  - **The debate:** one camp says populations near the eruption almost vanished. The other says some communities survived fairly close by.
  - **Southern Kyushu:** populations were already shrinking before the eruption, and only a few mobile foragers were left. This weakens the simple "volcano wiped them out" story.
  - **Northern Kyushu:** growth continued through the disaster and after. Refugees may have contributed, with people moving to coasts where sea life recovered quickly.
  - **Far from the volcano:** growth stalled or reversed. Suggested causes are a short climate shock, heavy tooth wear in deer and boar (possibly from grazing on ash), and health effects. These are possibilities, not demonstrated.
  - **How strong is this?** It's moderate and indirect. I couldn't open the paper to confirm what population measure they used (often counts of radiocarbon dates or sites, which have known biases).
  - ⚠️ The older "cultural break in southern Kyushu pottery" story is also from my background knowledge and unchecked.

## (4) Monitoring, and what "refilling" means
- ⚠️ **Standard volcanology, not checked this session:**
  - Calderas are watched for earthquakes, ground movement (GPS and satellite radar), gas output and heat.
  - At Kikai, most of the system is underwater, so seafloor instruments matter.
  - Warning signs of an eruption usually build over weeks to months: earthquake swarms, uplift, rising gas.
- **What this study does and doesn't show:**
  - It's one seismic snapshot that describes a structure. It doesn't measure change over time.
  - "Refilling" here means rebuilding over thousands of years.
  - A body that's only 3–6% melt is mostly solid, hot crystal mush. Magma generally has to be much more molten to erupt (background knowledge).
  - The authors' proposed indicator is repeating seismic surveys and tracking the change.

## (5) Things to avoid

- **"About to erupt" or "overdue":** no source says so, and volcanoes aren't on schedules.
- **"Supervolcano" stated as fact:** strictly, that term usually means VEI 8. K-Ah was VEI 7, so call it a giant caldera.
- **"Huge pool of liquid magma":** it's mostly solid mush, 3–6% melt.
- **220 km³ as a measured volume:** it's modelled from one line plus a symmetry assumption.
