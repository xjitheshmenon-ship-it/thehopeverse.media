## Numbers I could confirm
- **Heart-rate latency (time to maximum heart rate):** the median was 14.7 s for angry faces and 29 s for happy faces (Wilcoxon z = −2.39, p = 0.015). This is from the authors' reply (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5046936/).
- **Without the fastest horse:** the medians were 17.7 s and 29.10 s (p = 0.03), so the result holds.
- **Left-gaze bias for angry faces:** t(27) = 3.49, p = 0.002 (same reply).
- **Design:** each horse was tested twice, once per emotion, at least 2 months apart. Heart rate was recorded with a Polar Equine monitor (Sussex blog, https://blogs.sussex.ac.uk/psychology/2016/04/04/horses-read-human-facial-expressions-of-emotion-sussex-research/).
- **Not found:** absolute bpm values and photo dimensions. Don't put a bpm figure in the Short.

## Named horse
The authors' reply mentions one subject by name, **"Jack"**. His heart rate peaked in 1.6 s, the only latency under 5 s. He is a data point, not a star of the study, so I'd treat him as a colour detail at most. Don't invent a personality for him.

## Honest caveats (strongest hook for the Short)
1. **The result was challenged.** Tim Schmoll (Bielefeld) re-analysed the data in Biology Letters, 30 Sep 2016 (https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsbl/article/12/9/20160201/87908/Can-horses-read-emotional-cues-from-human-faces-Re). He argued the left-gaze result for angry faces can't be told apart from a horse reacting to any face, or to a stressful test setup. He also raised that the experimenter always stood on the horse's left. He found no significant difference between angry and happy in the probability of looking first with the left eye.
2. **The authors replied.** They said the left-gaze result for angry faces, the heart rate and the stress behaviours all point the same way. They also said the handler's position and test stress applied equally to the control trials. Their own reply says there was "no significant lateralized response to happy". It also says there was no significant difference between angry and happy in the gaze measure. So "looked more with the left eye at angry than at happy" overstates it. The solid contrast is the heart-rate timing.
3. **Small and narrow sample.** There were 28 horses, two men and photographs only. The Short should not say horses recognise anger in all people or in real life.
4. **The 2018 Current Biology paper** has also had methodological criticism. A commentary in *Learning & Behavior* (2019) calls it raising "important questions" despite "some methodological shortcomings" (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30338460/). I read only the abstract.

## Why it happens (both explanations are from the authors, not proven)
McComb offered two ideas. Horses may have adapted an ability to read other horses' emotional cues, or each horse may have learned human expressions in its own lifetime (Sussex release). Smith said recognising angry faces may work as a warning system, letting horses anticipate negative human behaviour such as rough handling (EurekAlert, https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/573334). The left-gaze pattern has also been documented in dogs (sci.news, https://www.sci.news/biology/horses-can-read-human-emotions-03627.html).

## Confirmed unique fact for the Short

It is the most robust result, and the critic's re-analysis did not remove it. It comes from the authors' reply (PMC5046936).
