## 4. The physics, simply

**Wave height vs run-up**
- Wave height is the vertical distance from a wave's crest to its trough, out on the water.
- Run-up is the highest point above sea level that the water reaches on land.
- On a steep wall the water's speed carries it upward, so run-up can be several times the wave height: here about 100 m of wave produced 481 m of run-up.
- A rough check, ignoring friction: to climb 481 m, water needs to be moving at about 97 m/s (roughly 350 km/h), from v = √(2gh).

**Why landslide tsunamis can be much taller locally**
- An earthquake tsunami comes from the seafloor shifting by a few meters over hundreds of kilometers. That makes a low wave, hundreds of kilometers long, carrying huge total energy.
- A landslide dumps a large mass into one spot almost instantly and pushes the water aside directly. The paper's authors attribute the extra height to this direct push, plus big local changes in water depth, plus confinement in narrow fjords.
- At Tracy Arm the opposite wall was only 1.6 km away, so the wave hit it before it had time to spread out.

**Why they fade quickly**
- The wave spreads outward from a single point, so its energy thins out fast.
- Its wavelengths are short, and short waves separate into a spread-out train of smaller waves as they travel.
- The total energy is far smaller than a big earthquake tsunami. That's why Tracy Arm was devastating nearby but only meant swamped gear and currents tens of kilometers away, while earthquake tsunamis can cross whole oceans.
- In a closed fjord, though, energy can be trapped and slosh back and forth (the seiche).

• Who was there: no injuries were reported. Kayakers lost gear, and one cruise ship had left about 12 hours earlier.
