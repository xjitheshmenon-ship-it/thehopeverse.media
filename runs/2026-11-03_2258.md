## Verified facts

**Owner and place**
- Hans was a horse owned by Wilhelm von Osten, a gymnasium maths teacher, amateur horse trainer and phrenologist. Wikipedia is the source I opened.
- The horse's exhibitions were in Germany in the early 20th century. Wikipedia gives c. 1894 to c. 1916 for his life.
- The NYT ran "Berlin's Wonderful Horse" on 4 Sept 1904. I only saw this citation listed in Wikipedia.
- **Not confirmed:** I did not confirm that the exhibitions were in Berlin itself. The NYT headline and the commission's Berlin zoo director point there, but Wikipedia says only "Germany".

**What Hans appeared to do**
- He could answer questions with about 90% accuracy by tapping his hoof, and seemed to add, subtract, multiply, divide and tell the time and date. This comes from fs.blog.
- FutureLearn says he seemed to have the maths skills of a 14-year-old. Damn Interesting calls that "by some estimates".
- He could answer anyone's questions, even with von Osten absent. This is why people ruled out simple fraud.

**1904 commission**
- A panel of 13 people formed the "Hans Commission". It included a vet, a circus manager, a cavalry officer, schoolteachers and the director of the Berlin zoo. Wikipedia says it was formed by the German board of education, with the philosopher and psychologist Carl Stumpf.
- It concluded in September 1904 that no tricks were involved.
- It then passed the evaluation to Oskar Pfungst, Stumpf's assistant.

**Pfungst's 1907 findings** (Wikipedia, Whole Dog Journal, FutureLearn, Damn Interesting)
- He isolated horse and questioner from spectators, used questioners other than von Osten, used blinkers, and varied whether the questioner knew the answer.
- Hans still answered correctly when someone other than von Osten asked, so this was not deliberate fraud.
- He was right only when the questioner knew the answer and he could see the questioner.
- With a questioner who didn't know the answer, or who was hidden, accuracy fell to nearly zero.
- Accuracy also dropped when the questioner stood farther away than usual.
- As the taps neared the right number, the questioner's posture, breathing and expression tensed. The tension released at the correct tap, and that was Hans's cue to stop. The fs.blog piece adds that many people tilted their head at that moment.
- Pfungst concluded von Osten sincerely believed in Hans.
- **Numbers:** Wikipedia says 89% (50 of 56) with Hans able to see the questioner, and 6% (2 of 35) when he could not. Whole Dog Journal gives the same 89% and 6%, but it describes them as von Osten knowing versus not knowing the answer. That is a different framing of the same numbers, and the two sites are probably not independent.
- **Safe wording:** "environ 89 % quand il voyait le questionneur, environ 6 % quand il ne le voyait pas."

**Legacy**
- Wikipedia says the effect led to double-blind experiments, and to replacing the experimenter with a computer that gives standardised instructions.
- In AI, "Clever Hans" describes an algorithm that seems to make correct predictions by using incorrect reasoning, and Explainable AI research tries to detect it. This is also from Wikipedia, so I have only one source for it.

**Balanced take**
- Smith, Proops, Grounds, Wathan and McComb (Sussex) published in *Biology Letters* on 10 Feb 2016.
- They studied how 28 horses reacted to photos of positive versus negative human faces.
- Horses looked longer with the left eye at angry faces, and Sussex reported a quicker heart-rate rise. ScienceDaily is the source for this.
- **Disputed:** A 2016 re-analysis in *Biology Letters* (Bielefeld University, PMC) says that, taking the other heart-rate measures together, there is no evidence of biologically relevant heart-rate effects. Say "une étude suggère", not "prouvé".
- Safe line: Hans did not count, but he read people extremely well.

**Senior-friendly comparison (my suggestion, not sourced)**
- Compare it to a card-game partner who senses from your face when you hold a good hand, or a grandchild who knows from your sigh when to stop asking.
