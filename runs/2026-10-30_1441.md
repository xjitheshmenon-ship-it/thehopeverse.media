## What makes it remarkable

The authors say the species is unknown to use tools. Auersperg says this shows tool craftsmanship can emerge from intelligence not specialised for tool use. The tool maker is also a *parrot* with a curved beak, not a crow or an ape.

## Honest caveats

1. **One bird.** In 2012 Figaro was alone in the species and among parrots in showing this. A 2022 commentary on the wild study still calls the wild finding based on a single case study.
2. **Tool use is not universal in the species.** In the wild-bird study (O'Hara et al., *Current Biology*, 31 Aug 2021), only two of the 15 wild-caught birds ever made and used tool sets. The highlights say only a few individuals do it.
3. **Correction to the brief.** The 2021 birds were wild-caught on Tanimbar and tested in an aviary during a capture-release study. They were not filmed in free flight. The same commentary says the behaviour had not been seen in the wild despite extensive field observation. So do not say "wild cockatoos use tools" without that nuance.
4. **The 2021 study weakens the "pure genius" reading.** The commentary suggests some innate predisposition for tool use. The 2021 birds used up to three tool types, so the species can do more than Figaro showed.
5. **The 2014 follow-up** shows tool use can spread socially, but only males learned it and the mechanism was emulation, not copying.

## Fair human comparison

Tool-innovation tests with children use the "hook task": bend a pipe cleaner into a hook to lift a basket from a tube.
- Studies show that before 8 years of age, children struggle to invent even simple tools on their own.
- In one task, fewer than 10% of UK children under 5 succeeded.
- In the Beck et al. task, the majority of children were successful only from age 8.

This is a different task, so say "children typically manage a comparable invention around age 8". Don't say Figaro is equivalent to an 8-year-old.
